## B. Hard operational risks the spec glosses over

### B1. GTT stops are not real stops

This is the most dangerous overstatement in the document. Your Strategy 2 runs 100% of capital in one stock with a GTT as the only defense. But per Zerodha's own terms: GTT triggers will be triggered only based on the live last traded price it is tracking on the exchange. When using GTT with limit price, there is no guaranteed execution as the LTP could have changed when the order is placed post trigger. Worse, Every GTT TSL is a one-time trigger; if it is sent to the exchange and fails to execute or gets rejected due to dynamic circuit limits, it will not reset or activate again.

Once triggered, it remains valid for the remainder of the trading day as a CNC (Longterm) limit order with Day validity. At the end of the day, the exchange treats it like any other normal day order and cancels it. So a gap-down through your stop can leave you with a cancelled order and a naked position: The GTT only sends a limit order at trigger, so in a violent gap the market can blow straight through your level and your order may not execute at all.

Additional gotchas: Zerodha does not verify Your account balance or available margin when you create a TSL order. Margins are checked only at the exact moment the order is triggered. And at some brokers, GTTs for shares having corporate actions like a bonus, dividend, stock split, rights, amalgamation, etc., having greater than 5% of market value are canceled on the ex-date/record date.

**Required fixes:**
- Stop GTTs must have the limit price set materially *below* the trigger (e.g., trigger −6%, limit −9% or lower) so the order is marketable after a gap.
- Add a **daily reconciliation job**: for every open position, assert an active GTT exists; if a trigger fired and the order died at EOD, re-arm or market-exit at next open. This is a code-level invariant, not a nice-to-have.
- Add a corporate-action calendar check so stops aren't silently cancelled on ex-dates.
- Delete the phrase "zero slippage" from Strategy 6. ₹5 Cr ADTV does not guarantee that.

### B2. The ₹26 cost buffer is arithmetically wrong

Costs scale with trade value, not per order. Equity Delivery: 0.1% on buy value + 0.1% on sell value. Plus 0.015% on equity delivery buy orders stamp duty, and Zerodha charges ₹13 per scrip as DP (depository participant) charges on the sell side of every equity delivery trade — flat, regardless of quantity. Brokerage itself is zero: Equity delivery is zero-brokerage — you only pay statutory charges (STT, exchange fees, SEBI fee, stamp duty) and a small DP charge if you sell.

On a ₹45,000 Tier-1 position, buy-side statutory cost alone is ≈ ₹52, already double your buffer. Round-trip friction is roughly 0.23–0.25% of notional. Replace `CMP ≤ Capital − ₹26` with `CMP × Q ≤ Capital × (1 − 0.0015)`.

Your Strategy 5 claims the regime filter "protects accumulated annual profits." The Indian evidence suggests you need more. The Nifty200 Momentum 30 index's recent drawdown ran 2024-09-27 · 2025-04-07 · - · -31.79% · 192 days, and historically, 2008 GFC : Nifty50 (-64.6%) | Nifty200M30 (-67.9%). ## 1. NSE Delivery Percentage — Your Single Best India-Specific Trap Filter

This is the most underused tool in your current spec, and it's unique to Indian exchanges. Delivery percentage measures what fraction of a stock's total traded volume on NSE actually resulted in delivery — shares that moved from one demat account to another rather than being squared off intraday. A high delivery percentage — typically above 60-65% — signals that a disproportionate chunk of the day's activity came from buyers and sellers with genuine conviction: institutions, HNIs, mutual funds, and long-term positional traders who are not reversing positions by 3:30 PM. Conversely, low delivery percentage indicates the day's volume was dominated by intraday operators and algo scalpers with no directional commitment.

- Narrow this to mid-cap and large-cap names above ₹2,000 crore market cap, since small-cap delivery data is easily distorted by thin float and a handful of bulk deals.
- Delivery % is descriptive, not predictive — a high reading confirms that today's move had real participation behind it, but it is not a forecast that the move continues. There's no universal threshold, but a jump in a stock's own delivery % versus its own historical norm is usually more telling than the absolute level.

**Why this matters for your 50-candidate problem:** it's a free, objective, NSE-published number (in the daily bhavcopy) that directly separates "real buyers took the stock home" from "day-traders flipped it." ## 2. Bulk & Block Deals — Naming the Buyer

Your current spec doesn't use this at all, and it should. A bulk deal happens when the quantity bought or sold is more than 0.5% of the total equity shares of a listed company in a single trading day, and these transactions are reported to the exchange and made public. A block deal is one trade of at least ₹25 crore in a separate window, and block deals must end in delivery while bulk deals can be reversed the same day.

The trap here: not all "deals" are bullish signals. A large, detailed analysis found 72% of NSE bulk deals since 2023 were same-day round trips, mostly by trading firms, with bulk deals clustering in small companies while large stakes change hands through block deals. And crucially, a block deal doesn't automatically mean bullish or bearish, because it can be a stake transfer between institutions, and similarly a bulk deal can be part of rebalancing rather than a directional bet.

**Practical rule:** Don't treat any single bulk/block deal as confirmation. Instead, track repeated bulk purchases across several sessions, since multiple bulk buys over several days may indicate institutional accumulation — this repeat-buyer pattern is far more reliable than a one-off print, and directly filters out the 72% round-trip noise.

## 3. Wyckoff Spring / Upthrust — The Classic Structural Trap Detector

This predates both Minervini and ICT and is directly applicable to your daily-bar screener. The core mechanism: an upthrust is the bearish mirror of the spring — price pushes above the resistance of a trading range, finds no real demand, and falls back inside; the pop above the highs fills resting buy stops and attracts breakout buyers, and its swift failure shows that higher prices could not be sustained.

The key diagnostic is volume-vs-close behavior: an upthrust is an effort vs result statement — volume expands into new highs yet the bar closes back inside the range, effort with no upward result. Wyckoff's own framing makes clear this is exactly a bull trap: a UT or UTAD is a price move above trading-range resistance that quickly reverses and closes in the range — a test of remaining demand, and also a bull trap that appears to signal resumption of the uptrend but is intended to wrong-foot uninformed breakout traders.

The distinguishing test between a real breakout and a trap is the close and retest, not the wick: a push above resistance that holds and builds acceptance is a breakout, not an upthrust, and the difference is often clear only from the close and the behavior on the retest.

**Direct rule for your screener:** For every one of your 50 candidates, check 2-3 days *after* the breakout bar — did the close hold above the prior resistance/pivot, or did price wick through and fall back inside the base? This single rule eliminates a large share of mechanical false breakouts that a same-day volume/RSI/VCR filter cannot see, because traps only reveal themselves on the *following* session's close.

## 4. ICT / Smart Money Concepts (Liquidity Sweeps) — Useful Concept, Limited Direct Fit

ICT is real and widely used, built on the idea that retail stop losses are institutional entry liquidity — when a long position gets stopped out, that selling is being bought by an institution using the stop loss as its entry. The mechanism: a liquidity sweep happens when smart money briefly pushes price beyond a key liquidity level to trigger retail stop orders before reversing the market.

**Important caveat for your use case:** ICT/SMC is predominantly an intraday/lower-timeframe (forex, futures, crypto) methodology built around session opens, order blocks, and fair value gaps — concepts like the opening-range sweep in the first 30-60 minutes of the New York session don't map cleanly onto an EOD (15:30 IST) swing/positional screener running on daily bars. The *conceptual* takeaway is directly usable, though — Wyckoff's spring/upthrust and ICT's liquidity sweep are the same phenomenon described by different schools, and other frameworks describe the same footprint as a sweep of buy-side liquidity or a false breakout of the range highs. For your daily-bar system, Wyckoff's framing (point 3 above) is the directly applicable version of this idea; full ICT entry mechanics (order blocks, FVGs, session-based sweeps) are generally overkill/mismatched for an EOD NSE cash equity screener.

## 2. The seven "genuineness" tests (codeable from EOD data)

### Test A — Base geometry: successive contractions + higher lows
Minervini: each contraction must be smaller than the one before it. If C2 is larger than C1, volatility is *expanding*, not contracting — the pattern fails, and the setup should be abandoned. Qullamaggie wants the same thing from a different angle: higher lows are particularly important because they indicate that buyers are entering at progressively higher prices. He also notes many high-quality setups produce one or more inside days immediately before the breakout, showing that price has become compressed into an exceptionally narrow range.

**Code:** detect swing lows over the last 40–65 sessions (zigzag with ~3% threshold). Require: depth(C_n) < depth(C_{n−1}) for each leg; final contraction depth ≤ 8%; last 5-day high-low range ≤ 1.5× ATR₁₄. Veto V-shaped bases — Morales/Kacher: Do not buy pocket pivots if the stock formed a 'V' where it sells off hard down through the 10dma or 50dma then shoots straight back up in a 'V' formation. Such formations are failure prone.

### Test B — Volume dry-up (VDU) *before* the breakout
This is arguably the single most important thing your spec lacks. You check volume on the breakout day, but not that supply dried up before it. A stock showing perfect progressive contractions but with high volume on pullbacks signals continued selling pressure - not accumulation completion. Volume dry-up confirms what price structure suggests. The visual tell: volume bars so low in that final contraction that you wonder if there was a trading halt or half-day session.

**Code:** `VDU = mean(Vol, last 5 sessions before breakout) / SMA(Vol, 50) ≤ 0.60`. Also: mean volume on down days inside the base < mean volume on up days inside the base (accumulation signature).

### Test C — Breakout bar character (the trap detector)
Three sub-tests, each from a different school, all pointing the same way:

1. **Closing range.** Strong breakouts close near the high of the day, demonstrating sustained buying pressure rather than intraday reversal. Wyckoff defines the trap as the mirror image — an Upthrust: Move above resistance on low volume, weak close - Look for: Low volume, close in lower half of bar. **Code:** `(Close − Low)/(High − Low) ≥ 0.70` to pass; `< 0.50` = Upthrust veto.

2. **Pocket-pivot volume test** (Morales & Kacher). Rather than comparing to a 20-day average, compare to recent *selling*: an up day, inside or emerging from a constructive base, whose volume exceeds the largest down-day volume of the previous ten trading sessions. The idea is that institutional buying leaves a volume footprint that stands taller than any recent selling. This is smarter than your 1.5× rule because it calibrates the footprint test to the stock's own recent selling: a quiet base needs only a modest print to qualify, while a volatile one demands real participation. **Code:** `Vol_today > max(Vol on down-close days, last 10 sessions)`.

3. **Benchmark period.** Use the 50-day average, not 20-day. Minervini's minimum is Breakout-day volume must be at least this multiple of the 50-day average. 1.4 = 40% above average (Minervini's minimum). A 20-day average inside a quiet base is artificially low, so your 1.5× threshold is easier to pass than it looks.

### Test D — Distance from *pivot*, not from SMA50
Your over-extension gate (25% above SMA50) is far too loose for entry timing. Minervini's scanners block entry when price is already too far above the pivot (no chasing). One implementation sets Breakout only when, on the snapshot session itself, the close is 0–3% above the pivot and that day's volume is at least 1.5× average. Qullamaggie's version: Avoid buying when stock is up more than ATR: Wait for pullbacks or consolidation before entering.

**Code:** `Pivot = max(High, final contraction)`. Deployable only if `0% ≤ (Close − Pivot)/Pivot ≤ 5%`. Beyond that → "Extended — wait for pullback to 10/20 EMA" (which is a *different*, valid entry; see Wyckoff LPS below).

### Test E — RS line at new high (true leadership)
I flagged this in the previous audit as the missing Trend Template criterion; the research reinforces it: Minervini says to look for stocks already demonstrating leadership — VCPs forming in weak stocks fail at a significantly higher rate than those forming in market leaders. Qullamaggie's 5-star setup explicitly requires RS at new high (market leader). **Code:** `RS_line = Close / Nifty500_close`. Pass if RS_line is at a 60-day high on or before the price breakout. RS line making a new high *before* price does is the strongest tell.

### Test F — Prior power move (the "something is happening" test)
Qullamaggie wants a stock already up 30 to 100% or more over the past one to three months, because the premise is that whatever caused that move has not finished playing out. Your Stage-2 rule (≥30% above 52-week low) is a weak version of this — a stock can be 30% above a low from 11 months ago and be dead. **Code:** `max(ROC_1M, ROC_2M, ROC_3M) ≥ 30%` as a scoring bonus, *then* tight base.

### Test G — Post-breakout confirmation (the cheapest trap filter of all)
The first day or two after breakout proves critical. The stock should hold gains and potentially continue advancing. If price immediately reverses back below the pivot point on heavy volume, the breakout likely fails. Most retail traps resolve within 1–3 sessions. The fix is not a filter but an **execution change**: enter 50% on the breakout-day signal, add 50% only if the stock closes above pivot for 2 consecutive sessions; exit everything on a close back below pivot regardless of your −5/−7% stop. This cuts the average loss on traps roughly in half.

## 3. The "trap school": Wyckoff and ICT

You asked about ICT, so a direct assessment. Wyckoff is the origin of all trap theory. The two mirror-image events:

- **Spring** (bullish): False breakdown below support - Look for: Low volume, quick reversal, close above support. Then confirmation: Wyckoff practice waits for confirmation: a quiet test that holds above the spring low, then a sign of strength on expanding volume. If price later closes back below the spring low, the accumulation read is invalidated.
- **Upthrust / UTAD** (bull trap): a false breakout *above* resistance that fails and reverses, the exact mirror of the accumulation Spring. Where a Spring traps sellers before markup, a UTAD traps buyers before markdown.

The textbook Wyckoff entry is actually *not* the breakout — it's the pullback after it: LPS—last point of support, the low point of a reaction or pullback after a SOS. Backing up to an LPS means a pullback to support that was formerly resistance, on diminished spread and volume. This is directly useful for your "Extended" bucket: instead of discarding a stock that broke out 8% above pivot, watch for the low-volume pullback to the pivot/20 EMA and enter there with a stop under the pullback low.

**ICT (Inner Circle Trader)** — my honest take: it is an intraday forex/index-futures framework (liquidity sweeps, order blocks, fair value gaps, "kill zones") built around session timing that doesn't map onto NSE EOD swing trading, and there's no independent evidence base for it. The one concept worth stealing is the "buy-side liquidity sweep," which is Wyckoff's upthrust renamed: price runs the obvious high (where retail stops and breakout orders cluster), fills institutional sells, and closes back below. The practical rule it gives you is a **"burnt pivot" veto**: if in the last 10 sessions the stock already closed above the pivot and then closed back below it, that pivot has been swept — demand a new base before re-entry. Skip order blocks and FVGs; they add nothing on daily bars that a VDU and closing-range test don't already capture.

## 4. India-specific institutional footprint (your biggest untapped edge)

### Delivery percentage — relative to the stock's own baseline
Everyone cites delivery %, but the right use is the anomaly, not the absolute: This screen specifically isolates stocks where delivery percentage has spiked significantly above their own 10 or 20-day average delivery percentage — not just high in absolute terms, but anomalously high relative to that stock's historical norm. Baselines differ hugely by segment: Speculative micro-caps typically run 10–20%, average mid-caps 30–45%, large-cap blue chips like HDFC Bank and TCS sit between 40–60%, and stocks under serious accumulation cross 60%. **Code:** `DelivZ = (Deliv%_today − mean(Deliv%, 60d)) / std(Deliv%, 60d)`. On the breakout day, require `DelivZ ≥ +1.0` and `Deliv%_today ≥ 30%`. Hard veto: `Vol_ratio ≥ 1.5 AND Deliv% < 20%` — high volume with low delivery is the classic operator/retail-churn signature. Also track the trend: if a stock's average creeps up over *two to three months*, you're watching genuine investor accumulation. One caveat — a single bulk deal can distort one day's figure, so use the z-score over 60 days, not one print.

### Bulk and block deals — mostly noise unless you net them
This one surprised me. A 90,000-row analysis found that In 72% of bulk deals, the same client bought and sold the same stock on the same day. The structural difference: Block deals must end in delivery; bulk deals can be reversed the same day. So raw "bulk deal buying" is nearly worthless as a signal. What works is netting out round-trips and counting distinct buyers: stocks where at least four distinct entities bought, buyers outnumbered sellers, and the disclosed deals were net buying by both value and quantity. Conversely, a single seller distributing a large block to many smaller buyers scores well on headcount while actually being an exit.

**Code:** from the NSE bulk-deal file, compute per (date, symbol, client) net value; drop rows where |net| < 20% of gross. Score +1 if ≥ 3 distinct net buyers in the last 20 sessions; veto if a single entity appears on both sides repeatedly (operator signature).

Not from the search results, but standard practice: FII + DII holding rising QoQ, promoter holding flat or rising, promoter pledge < 20%. A stock whose promoters are pledging or selling into a "breakout" is distribution dressed as accumulation.

The mechanism matters for trap detection: technical-only traders identify a perfect VCP pattern on a stock with deteriorating earnings, wondering why the breakout fails immediately.
